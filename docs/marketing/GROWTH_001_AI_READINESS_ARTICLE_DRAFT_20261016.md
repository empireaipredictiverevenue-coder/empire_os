# AI Readiness Assessment for UK Businesses: Cost, Scope and ROI

An **AI readiness assessment** helps a UK business decide whether an operational problem is suitable for AI, what evidence supports that decision, and what a realistic adoption plan should consider. It is not an automatic recommendation to buy AI software. This guide explains the questions to ask, the risks to review and how to compare a proposed assessment with other options.

## What is an AI readiness assessment, and who needs one?

A readiness assessment is an evidence-based review of processes, systems, data, operating constraints and decision ownership. It should start with a practical business question: which workflow has measurable cost, delay or risk, and can a proposed tool improve that outcome without compromising safety or compliance?

The [UK government's AI Adoption Research](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-adoption-research/ai-adoption-research) interviewed **3,500 businesses** in February–May 2025 and reported that **16%** used at least one AI technology in its survey population. Among businesses using AI, **77% reported no change in revenue** from their use at that time. These are self-reported observations from the study, **not** evidence that AI cannot work, that specific projects failed, or that Empire AI has delivered better results. The practical lesson is to measure productivity, cost, useful output and *actual revenue* separately before committing to a deployment. One population-level survey cannot determine whether your business is ready to automate a process.

Ask for named processes and exclusions, the data or systems to be inspected, responsible reviewers, how unresolved risks will be recorded and which specific documents or demonstrations you will receive. ## Which business data and processes should you assess first?

Begin with one repeatable workflow: capture current task volume, time spent, exceptions, accuracy requirements, customer impact and the cost of existing failures. Keep identifiable customer data out of an initial public enquiry. Ask who can lawfully authorize access, whether systems have audit logs, and which outputs must remain subject to human review.

The [Office for National Statistics' October 2026 business insights](https://www.ons.gov.uk/businessindustryandtrade/business/businessservices/bulletins/businessinsightsandimpactontheukeconomy/8october2026) reported that approximately 30% of businesses surveyed in September 2026 were using at least one AI technology. This is a different survey and time period from the government's 2025-fieldwork adoption research, so their percentages should **not** be treated as directly comparable or evidence that one business is ready.

## How do you calculate potential AI implementation ROI?

Use an **assumption-driven business case**, not a guaranteed return. For a defined review period, start with independently measured task frequency and time saved, then subtract estimated licence fees, implementation effort, monitoring, exception handling and the cost of human quality assurance.

A transparent worksheet can show:

- Baseline annual task time = observed task count × measured minutes per task.
- Hypothetical labour value released = validated saved hours × relevant fully loaded hourly cost. Time saved is not necessarily payroll savings.
- Illustrative net operational value = demonstrably useful savings − recurring tool cost − implementation costs − ongoing review and exception handling.

If the inputs are guesses, the output must remain a **scenario**, not realized revenue or an audited saving. Document the assumptions, test them in a limited pilot, and include the possibility of a negative return.

### Worked ROI scenario: measurable time value, not guaranteed savings

Suppose a UK operations team processes **200 transactions per month**, and a trial suggests a system *might* save **10 minutes per transaction**. At an assumed, fully loaded hourly labour cost of **£25**, that is around **33.3 hours of time** and **£833** of *potential time value* per month. Suppose the AI software costs **£400/month**, human review and rework cost **£150/month**, and implementation cost amortised over the evaluation period is **£200/month**. The resulting illustrative difference is around **£83/month**, **before** allowing for errors, variability, downtime, taxes or any extra data-security cost. None of these are verified Empire customer outcomes or cash savings; replace every input with observed figures from your own operations.

A buyer should ask which hours actually become higher-value work, whether the alternative is ordinary software rather than AI, and how to stop a pilot when its exceptions outweigh its benefits. A positive spreadsheet scenario is not proof of realised profit.

### Evidence to collect before signing any assessment

| Decision | Minimum source or test | What remains unknown without it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Which workflow matters? | Timed examples and a named process owner | Whether staff actually spend time on the problem |
| What data is usable? | Data inventory, access rights, retention obligations | Whether the proposed system can operate safely and lawfully |
| Is AI the right intervention? | Side-by-side comparison against current tools and a no-AI option | Whether less complex fixes would be cheaper or safer |
| How will value be verified? | Baseline, pilot window, success threshold and quality checks | Whether model output changes real work or revenue |
| What happens on failure? | Exception-handling plan, accountable approver and stop rule | Whether operational and customer risks are controlled |

## What governance and risks should an assessment cover?

Review data security, privacy, allowed data transfers, output accuracy, model and vendor retention terms, human approval, failure recovery, staff impact and responsibility for decisions. For higher-risk workflows, the first useful output may be a risk register rather than an automation plan.

A trustworthy report should identify what has not yet been tested. Do not present vendor product demonstrations or simulated transactions as independent proof of improved customer outcomes.

## What happens after an AI readiness review?

A credible next step is a ranked list of possible experiments, each with a named decision owner, an observed baseline, anticipated benefits, explicit uncertainty, an evaluation budget and a stop condition. Then a pilot can establish whether a particular change works in the buyer's environment. A fully scoped deployment is a separate decision; a readiness review does not automatically authorize software access, purchases or outbound communication.
