# How to tell which companies in your total addressable market are actually ready to buy

A total addressable market (TAM) tells you who *could* buy. It doesn't tell you who has a reason, a budget or permission to buy *now*.

That distinction becomes expensive when a sales team has thousands of plausible accounts but no clear way to decide which deserves research, a conversation or a proposal. A company can match your ideal customer profile, hire a new executive and appear in an industry report without ever becoming a qualified opportunity.

The useful question is not “Can we score every business as a buyer?” It's **“What observable evidence would justify our next action with this specific business?”**

Here's a practical way to work through it.

## The five levels of buyer readiness

| Evidence level | What you actually know | What you can responsibly do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **1. Market fit** | The business appears to match the industry, geography, size or use case you serve. | Add it to a research universe. Don't claim it is shopping. |
| **2. Observable change** | A dated, attributable event suggests a potential new problem: a facility expansion, hiring for relevant responsibilities, an acquisition or a service launch. | Investigate whether that change creates a problem your offer can solve. |
| **3. Corroborated need** | Two or more independent, current sources support a specific operational challenge, with an identifiable business and relevant product fit. | Qualify the context. Check whether a contact route is genuinely tied to the right person and company. |
| **4. Declared buying interest** | Someone with an appropriate role has directly requested a demonstration, scope discussion, quote or relevant information, with the consent and context needed to respond. | Review the actual conversation and respond within the requested scope, subject to suppression and outreach controls. |
| **5. Commercial commitment** | The buyer, acceptance criteria, payable event, price, responsibilities and necessary approvals are documented. | Work through contracting, fulfilment and payment verification. Recognise revenue only when the required economic evidence exists. |

These are **editorial decision categories, not probabilities**. A business at level two has not been measured as “40% ready to buy”. The levels describe which kind of statement your evidence supports.

### A concrete example

Suppose a regional services company announces it is opening a second branch.

**You know:** a public company announcement describes an expansion.

**You do not know:** whether its executives have approved a software budget, whether they want help predicting sales or whether your company is an acceptable supplier.

A useful research action is to check whether the second branch creates specific forecasting, territory-planning or operational visibility questions. You can then verify the source, company identity, dates and relevance. That's better than assigning a “hot lead” label merely because an expansion was announced.

Now suppose an authorised company representative visits your own enquiry form and asks for a Predictive Revenue diagnostic, expressly agreeing to a reply about that request. That is stronger evidence of declared interest. It still isn't a signed order or an invoice paid.

## Signals worth investigating—and their traps

| Signal | Why it might matter | Common false-positive |
| --- | --- | --- |
| New location, territory or product line | Could change demand forecasts, capacity or pipeline coverage. | The project is already fully funded and staffed, with no external supplier need. |
| Hiring for sales operations or analytics | May indicate investment in measurement and workflow. | The hiring is replacement headcount, unrelated to a purchase. |
| Public procurement or tender | May reveal an explicit requirement and process. | The relevant deadline passed, the tender excludes your offer or the apparent buyer is not the contracting authority. |
| Customer or market research activity | Can suggest a business is evaluating options in a category. | The research is academic, competitor-related or from an anonymous person unrelated to the account. |
| First-party demo, proposal or scope request | Provides a direct, attributable request that may justify a reply. | The address is unverified, the request is spam or a previous opt-out forbids contact. |

None of these observations should be silently converted into a verified named person's purchasing intent. Be especially careful when one site is a **directory about businesses**, rather than the actual business: a directory owner or executive isn't automatically the decision-maker at every listed company.

## A research checklist you can use today

1. **Name the specific problem.** “Revenue is growing” is not a problem statement. “Forecast confidence has dropped across newly launched territories” could be—but only if someone has actually evidenced that concern.
2. **Confirm the business identity.** Compare the organisation's first-party website, legal name, location and source domains. A lookalike, directory or parent company may be a different entity.
3. **Record the exact signal and date.** Capture the page or original document, observation time, source and what the source *actually says*. Separate the observed fact from your hypothesis.
4. **Test product fit.** Does your service address the evidence-backed issue? Don't turn a local homeowner enquiry into a buyer for an enterprise revenue platform.
5. **Check who can legitimately speak for the buyer.** Confirm role and company binding. Generic contact forms, unbound addresses and web-scraped lists do not establish buying authority.
6. **Check suppression and permission.** A request to stop means stop. A reply to an existing enquiry is not permission for unrelated marketing. Never send because a research ranking happens to be high.
7. **Make the smallest justified next move.** It may be *research more*, *hold*, *ask for clarification* or *prepare a scoped proposal*. No evidence means no automatic outreach.

A good evidence record ought to let a second reviewer answer **who said what, when, about which company, and why the proposed next action is justified**—without trusting a black-box “intent score”.

## What a buyer-readiness workflow should report

For each account, you need a transparent trail: verified organisation, observable change, source date, relevant product hypothesis, evidence quality, missing facts, contact status and next permitted action.

A practical review might produce:

- **Research only:** the company and event are real, but no buyer need or person-bound contact has been verified.
- **Qualification review:** the use case appears relevant, and there is enough independent context to ask whether a real conversation is appropriate.
- **Buyer-requested conversation:** the buyer has asked to engage through an approved channel; respond to that request with clear limits.
- **Commercial evidence review:** terms, buyer capacity, price and acceptance are established; only then progress towards a transaction.

Keep **observed signals**, **commercial forecasts** and **realised revenue** in different fields. Even a well-validated forecast is not a bank receipt.

## Where Predictive Revenue fits

Revenue forecasting and buyer qualification are related, but they are not the same thing.

An organisation evaluating Predictive Revenue might want to understand forecast variance, pipeline coverage, region-by-region demand assumptions or how to document which signals informed a commercial decision. Those are sensible discovery questions—not claims that any particular algorithm has already improved that organisation's sales.

If you are reviewing potential software or advisory options, ask the provider:

- Which inputs are **observed facts**, estimated probabilities and assumptions?
- Can a reviewer trace an opportunity back to independent source evidence?
- What happens when data is incomplete, stale or associated with the wrong company?
- Are customer, contact and tenant permissions enforced before an action is taken?
- How do forecasts connect to genuinely accepted orders, fulfilment and settled revenue?

The right product comparison should show **working controls and real evidence**, not an invented conversion percentage.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can an intent score tell me who will buy next?

Not on its own. A score is useful only when the input evidence, timing, calibration and limitations are understood. A predicted probability does not create a person-bound buying request or contractual authority.

### Is a hiring announcement a sales trigger?

It can be a **research trigger**. It cannot independently prove a budget, project or intent to purchase a specific product.

### Should I contact every account that matches my target market?

No. Qualification, person-company identity, relevance, source rights, suppression and applicable communication rules are different checks. A large TAM doesn't eliminate them.

### How do I measure whether this approach works?

Follow accepted stages, not vanity counts: evidenced research candidate → legitimate buyer-requested conversation → qualified need → accepted commercial offer → verified fulfilment/payment. Preserve the source and uncertainty at every transition. Treat unknown outcomes as unknown.
