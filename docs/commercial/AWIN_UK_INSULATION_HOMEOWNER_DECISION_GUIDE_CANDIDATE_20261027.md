# Home insulation: How to decide what to improve first without creating damp problems

When a home feels cold, installing the thickest insulation you can find is not always the right first move. The solution depends on **where heat is escaping**, the condition and construction of the building, existing ventilation, and the consequences of making one part of the structure colder or less able to dry.

For example, insulating the floor of an unconverted loft is different from insulating a sloping roof in a loft bedroom. Filling a suitable cavity wall differs from insulating a solid Victorian wall. Before paying for materials, establish **which part of your home needs attention and how moisture will still escape afterwards**.

This guide is a practical set of questions to take to an installer or surveyor. It is not a substitute for a building survey or an insulation design.

## Start with an evidence-based home check

Write down the following before requesting quotes:

1. **Type of building:** Detached, semi-detached, terrace or flat? Rough age and any known extensions? Is it listed or within a conservation area?
2. **Where you notice discomfort:** Top-floor ceiling, external walls, draughty loft hatch, suspended wooden floor or an isolated cold room? Discomfort alone does not prove which element has the worst heat loss.
3. **Existing measures:** Look for records of loft insulation, cavity wall filling, flooring changes and prior retrofits. Check an Energy Performance Certificate where one exists, but treat its recommendations as a starting point rather than an inspection.
4. **Water and moisture:** Has there been roof leakage, penetrating damp, mould, rotten timber or persistent condensation? Identify and resolve sources of water ingress before sealing parts of the building.
5. **Ventilation:** Where are the working extract fans, window trickle vents, airbricks and planned fresh-air paths? Adding insulation can change condensation patterns, so ventilation is part of the design—not an optional afterthought.

If insulation might conceal damp, alter a structure, block ventilation or disturb old materials, obtain a qualified assessment rather than attempting exploratory cutting or drilling.

**Independent context:** [Energy Saving Trust — fixing damp and condensation](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/fixing-damp-and-condensation/) and [Citizens Advice — finding an installer](https://www.citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer/energy/energy-supply/save-energy-at-home/find-an-installer-for-energy-efficiency-home-improvements/).

## Decide which part of your home you're actually insulating

| Building element | When it's worth investigating | Essential pre-installation check | Common mistake to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cold loft / roof space** | The loft is unconverted, with an insulated ceiling underneath | Existing insulation depth and condition, roof leaks, loft airflow, downlights, wiring, water pipes, tanks | Covering intentional eaves ventilation or unprotected electrics |
| **Sloping roof / loft room** | The loft is a heated living space | Roof build-up, ventilation, condensation control, fire performance and available headroom | Treating it like an unoccupied loft floor |
| **Cavity wall** | Survey confirms a suitable cavity and exposure to wind-driven rain is acceptable | Cavity size/condition, existing fill, bridging, damp and ventilation | Filling a narrow, unsuitable or rain-exposed cavity |
| **Solid wall** | Walls have no cavity or cavity insulation isn't appropriate | Moisture movement, wall condition, internal finish, heritage constraints, cold bridges | Choosing a material solely because it has the lowest advertised thermal conductivity |
| **Suspended timber floor** | The property has a ventilated void beneath floorboards | Timber condition, subfloor airbricks, access, pipes and services | Blocking air movement that keeps joists dry |
| **Pipes, loft hatch and draughts** | Small known heat-loss paths remain | Material safety, accessible services, watertightness and appropriate sealing | Sealing a purpose-built ventilation opening |

The table is a prioritisation aid. A professional should determine the actual sequence of measures, particularly for older or moisture-sensitive construction.

## Cold lofts: What to ask before adding rolls

For many suitable homes, loft insulation is an accessible upgrade. The material is commonly placed between and across ceiling joists, meaning the loft itself usually becomes **colder** because less heat rises from the rooms below.

That colder roof space needs appropriate ventilation. Warm, moist air reaching a poorly ventilated loft can condense on roof surfaces and timber. A good specification considers eaves ventilation and any extra provision required, rather than simply ordering rolls by the square metre.

Before buying materials, ask:

- Is the existing loft insulation dry, continuous and in good condition, or should damaged sections be investigated?
- Does the proposed thickness require new raised boarding or walkways? Will stored belongings compress the insulation?
- What clearance is required around downlights, flues and other hot components? Who checks the electrics?
- Will the hatch be insulated and draught-sealed appropriately?
- Are the loft's pipes and water tanks insulated to protect against freezing as the space gets colder?
- How will intentional air paths at the roof edges remain unobstructed?

Typical guidance often discusses approximately **270 mm of mineral-wool loft insulation**, but the required solution depends on the product and roof construction. It is **not** a universal product prescription or fire/ventilation specification.

Read: [Energy Saving Trust — roof and loft insulation](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/roof-and-loft-insulation/).

## Cavity walls: Confirm that the wall is suitable

Cavity-wall insulation is installed between two layers of wall construction, where a suitable cavity exists. Not every brick wall has a suitable empty cavity. A survey must check for previous filling, defects, wall ties and the risk of water entering through the outer wall.

An installer should assess whether the property is exposed to driving rain, where there are cracks or failing pointing, and how existing wall and floor vents will remain functional. This matters because an unsuitable installation can worsen moisture problems.

Don't agree to a universal 'inject and forget' offer before an appropriately qualified professional has established that the specific wall construction can accept it. Ask for a written suitability and moisture assessment, proposed material, installation method and remediation responsibilities.

Read: [Energy Saving Trust — cavity wall insulation](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/cavity-wall-insulation/).

## Solid walls: Moisture and ventilation come before a shopping list

Older solid masonry can respond differently from modern cavity walls. Adding internal insulation may make the old wall colder, increasing the risk of interstitial condensation. External insulation can change a building's appearance, window details, eaves and drainage interfaces.

Wall assemblies need to be designed so that rainwater, ground moisture and water vapour are understood and managed. Some structures need vapour-permeable insulation and finishes; others require a different compatible system. A suitable designer should explain how proposed boards, fixings, membranes and finishes work together rather than treating one component's conductivity figure as proof of a safe installation.

Specific questions for your surveyor or installer:

- Are the walls dry and structurally sound, or do gutters, render, pointing or the roof need repair first?
- Is the building listed, in a designated area or protected by leasehold rules?
- What happens at window reveals, joist ends, skirting boards and junctions where insulation is interrupted?
- How will ventilation and moisture control be maintained?
- Does the work change room dimensions, access to services, thermal bridging or fire safety requirements?
- What commissioning, workmanship and long-term warranties apply?

Read: [Energy Saving Trust — solid wall insulation](https://energysavingtrust.org.uk/advice/solid-wall-insulation/).

## Choosing insulation materials: Compare a specification, not a slogan

A product marketed as 'high performance' still needs to suit the **exact location and build-up**. When comparing mineral wool, rigid foam boards or other insulation products, ask for the manufacturer's current technical data and suitability for your installation.

- **Thermal conductivity (lambda, W/m·K):** Lower values indicate less heat conduction *through the material*, but do not alone describe the finished building element.
- **Thermal resistance (R, m²·K/W):** Depends on material thickness as well as thermal conductivity. For a uniform material, approximate layer R = thickness in metres ÷ lambda. This is useful for comparing layers, **not** permission to self-design an entire wall.
- **U-value (W/m²·K):** Describes heat flow through an assembled building element. It accounts for more than a single product's advertised lambda. Lower U-values typically mean better insulation performance, but assessment must include build-up, bridges and air leaks.
- **Moisture compatibility:** Check vapour behaviour, exposure to rain, ventilation and drying potential in the actual assembly.
- **Fire and installation constraints:** Ask how the product's classifications and system approvals apply to the whole construction, fixings, penetrations and location; do not infer safe use from a retailer's short product description.
- **Environmental considerations:** Ask about verified product environmental information, longevity and the disposal or reuse implications of the proposed work.

When product values are missing or incomparable, mark them **Not provided** rather than filling gaps with assumptions.

## Next step: A practical order of decisions

**First**, identify where you believe heat is being lost, using bills, records, accessible visual checks and qualified inspection—not an assumed universal ranking. **Second**, resolve moisture, ventilation and structural problems before selecting products. **Third**, decide whether the project is appropriate for competent DIY work or needs a suitably qualified surveyor and professional installation. **Fourth**, compare three fully scoped quotations, taking safety, moisture management, workmanship and exclusions as seriously as a material's advertised thermal performance. **Finally**, check whether current support can reduce the actual installation cost.
